\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A monitor that flags harmful text only after it appears cannot stop it.
This matters more as language models write long reports, run multi-step agents and produce thousands of tokens per response.
A useful monitor would read the model while it generates and say, early, \emph{when} the response is likely to turn unsafe and \emph{what} the rest of the response will do.

Activations are a natural input for such a monitor.
Probes recover global properties of an upcoming response, such as its length, from the last prompt state~\citep{dong2025emergent}.
Activation verbalizers such as LatentQA~\citep{pan2024latentqa} and Activation Oracles~\citep{karvonen2025activation} read a fixed window of activations and answer free-form questions about the model, including properties of its future completion.
Streaming safety monitors~\citep{li2025scm,stream2026guard,qwen2025guard} act on partial generations.

{\bf What is missing?}
Every one of these readers answers about the present or about the whole next reply, without a time axis.
The longest activation-based lookahead we found in the literature is about 32 tokens~\citep{egtp2026}, and next-token-lens methods decay within a few tokens~\citep{pal2023futurelens}.
None keeps a memory of the growing activation trajectory, and none outputs a distribution over \emph{when} an event occurs.
Streaming safety monitors evaluate onsets that mostly occur within the first sentence and do not report performance by lead distance.

{\bf Our approach.}
We hypothesize that an observer with three properties forecasts further ahead than a direct readout trained on the same labels: (a) a streaming memory over the activation trajectory, (b) predicted latents for future windows, trained with a JEPA-style future-activation loss, and (c) an explicit discrete-time hazard head that handles right-censoring.
We build three observers with these properties (\figref{fig:method}): a horizon-query bottleneck (\hq), a latent rollout (\roll) and a hierarchical coarse-to-fine model (\hier).
We compare them against \directao, a matched readout of the latest activation with the same encoder, inputs, targets, parameter budget ($\approx$6M) and epochs, on a frozen \qwen.
We evaluate two tasks: forecasting the first unsafe (or caution) segment within $H \in \{64,\dots,2048\}$ tokens, and forecasting the behavior, topic and summary of future segments of long responses.

{\bf What do we find?}
The answer is weak and mixed.
Natural unsafe onsets on unmodified benchmark prompts are rare and early (5 of 84 trajectories, all $\le$448 tokens), so the primary safety event is unpowered and a text-only baseline wins on it.
On the denser caution event, \hier improves AUROC over \directao by $+0.04$ to $+0.11$ at every $H \le 1024$ (0.976--0.990 vs.\ 0.871--0.953), but confidence intervals touch zero, the gain peaks at $H=256$--$512$ rather than growing with $H$, and a linear probe is nearly as good.
Removing memory is the ablation that hurts most at long horizons; the future-activation loss, the hazard head and latent rollout bring no gain.
On long-form content, observers forecast future-segment behavior at 0.83--0.88 accuracy up to 1024 tokens ahead (majority 0.62) and retrieve the remaining-response summary at 4--5$\times$ chance MRR.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt,topsep=0pt]
    \item We formulate long-horizon activation forecasting as censored event-time prediction over lead distances up to 2048 tokens, with leak-checked inputs and a prompt-family split fixed before generation.
    \item We design three observer architectures that combine streaming memory, future-window queries, a future-activation auxiliary loss and a logistic hazard head, and compare them against a matched direct readout and five non-neural baselines.
    \item We run component ablations and zero/shuffled-activation controls, and show that memory is the one component with a consistent long-horizon benefit while the hazard head hurts long-horizon calibration under heavy censoring.
    \item We report the limits of natural data honestly: unmodified prompts give too few late unsafe onsets, and pooled AUROC largely measures prompt-type separation rather than timing.
\end{itemize}
